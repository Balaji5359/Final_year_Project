ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the problem of optimally assigning airplanes to airport gates (Airport Gate Assignment 
Problem — AGAP), with GHG emissions considerations. A multi-objective mathematical programming model is 
formulated with the objectives of minimizing flights assigned to apron gates, walking distances of connecting 
passengers, and GHG emissions because of airplane taxiing movements. A genetic algorithm, which incorporates 
a Nash equilibrium control element, is used to solve the problem. The Nash equilibrium element allows the fast 
and efficient identification of those genetic algorithm parameters that yield the best solution to the problem. The 
model and solution process are demonstrated in a hypothetical airport with ten gates, using simulated flight 
schedule data, which resemble operations of a real-world scaled-down airport with high passenger traffic. Results 
indicate considerable GHG emission savings and improved passenger walking burden at the airport over a 
simplified assignment approach. Specifically, the algorithm achieved a reduction of 1356.6 Kg CO2 emissions 
compared to non-optimized flight distribution, i.e. an average reduction of about 19 liters per aircraft, as well as 
an additional indirect reduction in total CO2 emissions due to a reduction in delays and congestion.
1. Introduction
The significant rise in air traffic over the years before the COVID-19 
pandemic period has increased complexity in airport ground operations, 
which, along with other factors, causes delays in airplane departures 
( Z ´amkov ´a et al., 2019 ). The COVID-19 pandemic had a devastating 
impact on the airline industry, causing a significant decline in airport 
traffic ( Sun et al., 2021 ; Wu et al., 2023 ). After the come back to sta -
bility, the rise continue and overload this period ( Sun et al., 2023 ). 
Among them, air carrier attributed delays because of late airplane ar -
rivals occur in about 63 % of delayed flights ( Bureau of Transportation 
Statistics, 2025 ); these delays are usually attributed to inadequate 
airport operations and passenger traffic management ( Eurocontrol, 
2022 , 2024 ). Areas of airports where delays occur include departure 
gates, taxiways, apron areas and runways, with taxiway-related pro -
cedures, runway usage, air and ground traffic congestion, weather 
conditions, and airplane malfunctions being major sources for them 
( Belobaba, 2009 ).
Airlines are also focusing on air service quality levels and passenger 
satisfaction. With delays, this is not possible because they often cause 
long queues at airports, long waiting times and therefore a not so 
positive experience for passengers, as they can be a major source of 
negative emotions and anger for passengers ( Kim & Park, 2016 ). Addi -
tionally, passengers need to be treated differently because of the diffi -
culty in achieving relative passenger comfort and avoiding problems 
such as missed connections, baggage that did not reach the final desti -
nation and passenger inconvenience. For this reason their responses 
need to have three basic things: speed, efficiency and reliability ( De 
Neufville & Odoni, 2013 ). Furthermore, air transportation is a GHG 
emission-intensive sector; even though air transportation is responsible 
for 2.5 % of global emissions ( Lee et al., 2021 ), only 10 % of the world ’ s 
population uses airplanes for travel ( G ¨ossling & Humpe, 2020 ). The 
outlook is not encouraging, since air transportation generated CO2 
emissions are expected to increase up to 22 % by 2050 ( European Union 
(EU) 2015 ).
The air transportation industry is under growing pressure to reduce 
emissions as its increasing contribution to global greenhouse gases 
conflicts with international climate goals as the net-zero emissions 
target by 2050 ( United Nations, 2021 ).
To achieve this, IATA estimates that it is essential to focus on more 
efficient and effective aircraft, using sustainable fuels ( Wandelt et al., 
2025 ), and implementing policies and strategies to reduce emissions

Assigning airplanes to departure gates is a critical process in airport 
ground operations, as it affects passenger services and operations. The 
so-called “Airport Gate Assignment Problem (AGAP)” is that of allo -
cating arriving airplanes to airport gates so that delays and operational 
costs are minimized while operational efficiency and a seamless pas -
senger experience are achieved ( Das ¸ et al., 2020 ). The flight gate 
assignment problem is guided by decision-makers’ priorities, which 
generally fall into two categories: passenger-oriented and 
airport-oriented. Passenger-oriented factors focus on service quality and 
satisfaction, including passenger travel distance ( Cheng et al., 2012 ; 
Ding et al., 2004 , 2005 ; Haghani & Chen, 1998 ; Marinelli et al., 2015 ; 
Yan & Chang, 1998 ; Yan & Huo, 2001 ), terminal response time ( Kim 
et al., 2013 ) baggage travel distance ( Haghani & Chen, 1998 ) and pas -
senger waiting times ( Yan & Huo, 2001 ). However, airport-oriented 
factors emphasize efficiency and cost reduction, such as flight apron 
assignments ( Ding et al., 2004 , 2005 ; Marinelli et al. (2015) ., flight-gate 
preferences ( Dorndorf et al. (2008 , 2012 ), and aircraft handling costs, 
including towing operations, gate reuse, and taxiing time ( Dorndorf 
et al., 2008 , 2012 ; Kim et al., 2013 ).
In this context, this paper proposes a model for the AGAP, which 
introduces the aspect of minimizing the carbon footprint of airplanes in 
the assignment process. Indeed, assigning airplanes to gates in such a 
way that aircraft waiting times, taxiing distances and transfer passengers 
walking distances are minimized may contribute to both improved op -
erations and lower GHG emissions ( Dissanayaka et al., 2020 ). The 
remainder of the paper is structured as follows: In Section 2 , a review of 
the literature is offered in the subject, while Section 3 presents model 
developed for the study and the analysis of methodology. Section 4
showcases an application of the model in a hypothetical airport layout, 
and conclusions are provided in Section 5 of the paper together that 
propose further research.
2. Literature review
The focus of the airport gate assignment problem (AGAP) is to 
improve the allocation of airplanes to airport gates and essentially 
minimize associated passenger and operator costs. Different mathe -
matical programming models have been introduced over the years for 
describing the AGAP, along with various techniques adopted for solving 
the associated mathematical programs ( Bouras et al., 2014 ). In an early 
study, Mangoubi and Mathaisel (1985) applied a linear programming 
model and a heuristic for assigning airplanes to gates so that the total 
walking distances for passengers were minimized. Yan and Chang 
(1998) formulated the GAP as a network model, which considered 
different operational constraints and Haghani and Chen (1998) devel -
oped mathematical programming models for the AGAP, which opti -
mized total walking distance for passengers and gate utilization. 
Multi-objective optimization models for gate assignment were pro -
posed by Yan and Huo (2001) , which considered passenger walking 
distances, gate utilization, and operational constraints. Dynamic 
assignment of gates to airplanes was tackled by Xu and Bailey (2001) ; 
their model was formulated as a mixed integer quadrating program, 
which attempted to minimize waiting times for both transferring and 
non-transferring passengers.
Ding et al. (2004 , 2005 ) investigated the over-constrained AGAP and 
attempted to minimize gate conflicts and improve gate utilization of 
gate resources, using heuristics and applying a greedy algorithm and a 
neighborhood search method for minimizing unassigned flights to a gate 
and passenger walking distance. Cheng et al. (2012) explored the 
application of meta-heuristics to the airport gate assignment problem 
AGAP) and concluded that meta-heuristics can produce efficient solu -
tions within a reasonable timeframe and Kim et al. (2013) developed a 
comprehensive scheduling framework that integrates various aspects of 
airport operations to optimize gate assignments. Minimizing passenger 
transit time and gate idle time was the focus of Marinelli et al. (2015) .
In a series of papers, by Dondorf et al. (2008 , 2012 ) the AGAP was 
modeled as a clique partitioning problem with an or on maintaining 
robust schedules concerning a reference schedule. Maharjan and Matis 
(2011) converted the AGAP into a multi-commodity flow network 
model, which minimized total walking distance for passengers during 
gate reassignment, and Kumar and Bierlaire (2014) modeled the AGAP 
by concentrating on connection revenues, minimizing zone usage costs, 
and increasing the robustness of gate assignments. An alternative 
approach based on quantum computers and quadratic unconstrained 
binary optimization for modeling the AGAP was proposed by Stollen -
werk et al. (2018) . A mixed integer linear programming model was 
developed by Silva et al. (2020) for the GAP, which considered maxi -
mization of airport revenues and passenger behavior. Similarly, Wen 
and He (2020) focused on passenger satisfaction during layovers for the 
AGAP by minimizing waiting times in their model.
Asadi et al. (2021) developed a hybrid meta-heuristic algorithm by 
combining the Shuffled Frog-Leaping Algorithm (SFLA) and the Grass -
hopper Optimization Algorithm (GOA). This approach overcame the 
drawbacks of each algorithm, achieving an optimal solution with 
reduced computational costs, and was tested using a scenario from 
Frankfurt Airport. Bi et al. (2022) proposed a Branch-and-Price algo -
rithm (BAPA) solution and improved the solution speed by adding a 
heuristic search with a certain probability in each search node and 
verifying the model’s performance by using actual flight data of 
Kunming Changshui International Airport, China. Karsu and Solyalı 
(2022) developed a mixed-integer linear programming model to mini -
mize apron usage and passenger travel distances within the airport. 
More recently, Shan et al. (2023) indirectly incorporated GHG emission 
considerations in their AGAP model, aiming to optimize flight assign -
ments, fuel consumption, and gate assignment robustness. Das and 
Gzara (2024) presented a column-generation-based algorithm to 
generate gate schedules that minimized squared slack time while 
reducing passenger walking distances. Recently, Cao et al. (2024) pro -
posed a more recent approach, introducing a predict-then-optimize 
framework; a CNN-LSTM-Attention model was used to predict flight 
arrival times and employed a bi-objective optimization model for 
scheduling. Published papers are summarized in Table 1 :
The concept of Nash Equilibrium offers a realistic and widely 
accepted solution framework for strategic games where there are no 
strictly dominant strategies. In a Nash Equilibrium, each player chooses 
a strategy that is an optimal response to the strategies of the others, with 
no incentive to unilaterally change it ( Nash, 1950 ). This equilibrium 
requires rational behavior, shared knowledge of rationality, and aligned 
expectations among players, ensuring stability of the system under in -
dividual deviations ( Osborne, 2002 ).
Overall, the rich literature on the AGAP largely focuses on maxi -
mizing passenger comfort, by minimizing walking distances traveled by 
passengers and layover times. Some studies focus on optimizing airport 
revenues, by minimizing luggage transportation distances, vacant gates, 
and the time that these remain without an assigned airplane, flight gate 
preferences, taxiing distances, and airplane handling costs. However, 
only a handful of studies indirectly consider the environmental impacts 
of ground operations related to assigning arriving airplanes to gates. In 
this context, this paper contributes to the literature by proposing and 
demonstrating a new model and a meta-heuristic model for representing 
and solving the AGAP. The model considers both passenger aspects 
walking distances for connecting flights) and GHG emissions produced 
because of airplane ground operations (taxiing).

3. Methodology
3.1. Model
Without loss of generality, the proposed model considers two plan -
ning objectives: passenger comfort and environmentally sustainable 
operations. The former is passenger-oriented and addressed by mini -
mizing the number of airplanes assigned to an apron gate and the 
walking distance of travelers in connecting flights ( Dorndorf et al., 
2007 ). Indeed, walking and/or using a bus to reach an apron gate, as 
well as walking to a connecting flight are factors strongly affecting the 
comfort of air travelers and it affects the possibility of being late for the 
next flight when the transferring time is short. The latter planning 
objective is introduced in this study, by attempting to minimize GHG 
produced during taxing. In this context, let:

The objective function (1) consists of three weighted components: 
the first component minimizes the total number of flights corresponding 
to gates at the apron. There are benefits to having flights loaded at gates 
in the terminal building instead of the apron: fewer flights in the apron 
area increase efficiency and facilitate passenger boarding, and there are 
no emissions from airport buses. The second component reflects the 
walking distance of travelers from their arrival to their connecting flight. 
The third component minimizes the total GHG emissions of airplanes 
moving from their landing spots to their assigned gates and from their 
assigned gates to the take-off positions for their next flight as a function 
of airplane consumption, taxiing distance, and idling time. Constraints 
(2) assign one gate per airplane simultaneously, and constraints (3) 
ensure that an aircraft can only be assigned to a gate and not be reas -
signed to a second gate. Constraints (4) set the minimum turnaround 
time, and constraints (5) ensure that airplanes whose turnaround tim -
ings overlap cannot be assigned to the same gate. Constraints (6) set the 
minimum time between successive airplanes assigned to a gate. Finally, 
constraints (7) are gate type and size constraints, which dictate re -
strictions on the sizes of airplanes that can be accommodated by a gate 
and whether a gate can accommodate flights from specific destinations 
(domestic, international).
2.2. Solution algorithm
A genetic algorithm (GA) is used for solving the model; GAs are 
selected because they offer a large solution space exploration, making it 
ideal for navigating complex and extensive search spaces. Assignment 
problems, such as AGAP, can be complex problems because of the large 
number of gates and flights. Also, the algorithm is well-suited for multi- 
objective optimization, enabling the simultaneous optimization of 
multiple criteria, such as taxiing distance and passenger comfort. 
Indeed, according to the literature review on the AGAP, several studies 
have exploited GAs for tackling the problem at hand. The proposed GA 
incorporates a Nash equilibrium control, which aids in both calibrating 
GA parameters and act as a local optimization mechanism. The rationale 
behind the Nash equilibrium in a GA is that of ensuring that no single 
parameter can unilaterally change its value to improve the overall GA 
performance while other parameters remain fixed.
The general structure of the GA with Nash control is straightforward: 
the algorithm starts by setting the GA parameters (number of genera -
tions, population size, etc.) and the problem data related to flights and 
gates. An initial population of candidate solutions is generated (popu -
lation initialization); to ensure these starting solutions are feasible, a 
solution repair process is performed concerning gate type and size 
compatibility, as not all aircraft are accepted at all gates, and time 
overlaps cannot occur. For that initial population of solutions, the 
objective function (1) is calculated, which acts as the GA candidate so -
lution fitness measure. The Genetic Algorithm (GA) selects parents 
based on fitness and performs crossover to exchange genetic informa -
tion, creating diverse offspring. Mutation is applied to introduce random 
changes, maintaining variety and preventing the algorithm from

rium control is then applied: for each flight, its possible assignment to 
every available gate (including space on the runway) is tested. At each 
trial, the fitness of the modified solution is recalculated. If there is even 
one case where changing the gate of a single flight leads to a better 
fitness function value, then the solution is not considered a Nash Equi -
librium. Conversely, if no gate change for any flight results in an 
improvement, the solution is regarded as being in Nash Equilibrium. 
Essentially, this process performs a local optimization check, assessing 
whether the solution is stable against individual changes, without 
requiring simultaneous adjustments for all flights. The application of the 
Nash control allows therefore to efficiently evaluate the performance of 
different GA parameters for the same number of generations, and also 
perform a local improvement of GA solutions for that number of gen -
erations. In terms of the gate assignment problem each flight is treated as 
a "player" making decisions about which gate to allocate, with the 
allocation influenced by the actions of other flights (i.e., other players). 
The Nash Equilibrium (NE) concept is applied as a form of local opti -
mization, where every small modification to the solution is evaluated. 
This ensures that the final solution cannot be easily improved through 
unilateral changes by any single flight. While the concept of Nash 
Equilibrium is referenced, the problem is not formally defined or solved 
as a game theory problem. Consequently, traditional game-theoretic 
mathematical formulations (e.g., payoff calculation) are not employed. 
The genetic algorithm ensures solution stability by verifying that no 
individual flight repositioning can improve the overall fitness of the 
solution. If a solution is in NE, no single flight can change its assignment 
without negatively impacting the overall system performance and 
fitness. The genetic algorithm (GA) terminates after a maximum number 
of generations or convergence of solutions is reached according to the 
Nash equilibrium control. Note that GHG emissions in the fitness func -
tion are calculated according to the fuel consumption of each aircraft 
while on the ground and the total taxi distance traveled, assuming an 
average taxiing speed and using appropriate conversion factors.
4. Application
4.1. Overview and data
To validate our model, we apply it to a hypothetical airport layout 
that accommodates domestic and international flights to test our 
methodology under controlled conditions. Our example airport is based 
on a scaled-down version of a real-world, high-passenger airport. That 
means that simulate the ratio of aircraft to gates of an airport with high 
airport traffic volume because we want to test out the model in difficult 
situations. These figures are comparable to Gatwick Airport, UK South 
Terminal, with a ratio of 1.71 ( London Gatwick Airport, 2025 ) and 
Newark Airport in New Jersey, whose Terminal A has a ratio of 2.03 
( Aviation Newark Liberty International Airport, 2025 ). For instance, 
Newark Liberty International Airport (EWR) in a typical four-hour time 
slot in one terminal, approximately 60 aircraft require gate assignments 
across 30 gates. To create a manageable yet representative case, we 
proportionally scale this scenario by a factor of three, resulting in an 
airport with 20 aircraft and 10 gates. This approach ensures that the 
complexity of the scheduling problem remains realistic while allowing 
for efficient testing and analysis. The airport has a single terminal with 
ten contact gates, an apron area, and one runway. A simulated, 
four-hour daily peak schedule is set up, with incoming flights assumed to 
arrive every 10 – 20 min during peaks. This enables us to capture realistic 
scheduling dynamics without excessive computational overhead. Flights 
are categorized as either domestic or international and arrive at the 
same terminal, with only some gates being allowed to handle 
international flights and while all flights use the same terminal, only a 
subset of gates is designated for international arrivals due to customs 
and security requirements. The airport accommodates three common 
aircraft design groups (III, IV, and V) based on FAA classifications 
( Federal Aviation Administration, 2024 ). These categories account for 
differences in aircraft length and wingspan. For reference, an Airbus 
A320 falls into Category III, while a Boeing 777 belongs to Category V. 
The fuel consumption estimation based on the basic fuel consumption 
value with further adjustment to the size of the aircrafts.
The model can be extended to incorporate additional complexities, 
such as longer connection distances in multi-terminal airports and 
enhanced security screening procedures for international arrivals.

The best objective function value and thus a high efficiency solution 
for the problem, to achieve the assignment of aircraft to gates with 
reduced GHG emissions, is given by the following combination: {4, 8, 2,

also provides the largest reduction in GHG emissions compared to the 
first-fit strategy assignment. Since the problem has three components for 
optimization, the best solution yields a minimum value for the three of 
them, and not just the one that leads to the greatest reduction in emis -
sions. Additionally, both the first and second components indirectly 
contribute to reducing emissions. Indeed, by reducing the number of 
flights assigned to the apron area, emissions from buses transporting 
passengers from the apron area to the terminal are reduced. Moreover, 
by reducing the distance passengers need to travel to reach their con -
necting gate, the time to get there is also reduced, as are the chances of 
departure delays. The algorithm reduced CO ₂ emissions by 1356.6 kg 
compared to unoptimized flight allocations, equivalent to an average 
fuel savings of about 19 liters per aircraft. For the calculation of CO2 
emissions, the Fuel Conversion Factor was used, which for the specific 
Jet-A fuel type is 3.16 Kg CO2/Kg fuel ( IATA, 2022 ).

Flight-to-gate assignments have been created based on scheduled 
flight times, and only minor delays have been considered during the gate 
allocation process. In the case of significant delays, reassigning flights to 
new gates becomes necessary, even though rerunning the schedule with 
the updated arrival/departure times may disrupt the scheduled gate 
assignments and impact passengers ’ comfort. To minimize the impact of 
flight delays, the following strategy is adopted: the gate assignments for 
all flights will remain unchanged up to the flight directly preceding the 
delayed one; subsequently, any affected flights will be reassigned to 
minimize deviations from the originally planned schedule, thereby 
reducing disruption. Considering that flight announcements on airport 
information boards are typically made 1 – 2 h before departure, the 
rescheduling process prioritizes minimal changes to the displayed 
schedule to ensure consistency and passenger convenience. For example, 
if flight N ο “ TT8890 ” has a 1.5-hour delay (new arrival time Table 6 ), 
the updated gate assignment will be the following: [4, 8, 2, 5, 7, 1, 4, 0, 
6, 5, 2, 8, 9, 2, 8, 7, 6, 5, 1, 0].
4.4. Sensitivity analysis
A sensitivity analysis was also performed to examine the impact of 
changing various factors on the optimization results ( Fig. 2 ). Indica -
tively, based on the analysis, when the distance between gates is reduced 
to half, the optimized GHG emissions are reduced to 6521 (26 kg 
decrease) and the optimized walking distance is reduced to 5875 m 
(3875 m decrease). Also, when the distance between gates is doubled, 
the optimized GHG emissions are reduced to 5926 kg (621 kg decrease) 
and the optimized walking distance is equal to 0 (9750 decrease). Based 
on the results, it is observed that, as the distance between gates is 
reduced, the importance of this criterion in the objective function is less 
significant. When the airport becomes significantly larger and the dis -
tance between gates increases, the walking distance equals zero, as it 
makes sense to keep passengers from transit flights at the same gates.
In terms of GHG emissions, there is no standard trend. These insights 
are valid for this case where the number of transfer passengers is limited 
(65 pax). Regarding the sensitivity analysis of the distances from the 
runway to the gate and vice versa, it is normal to observe that as the 
distances traveled by airplanes increase, CO2 emissions increase. The 
optimized taxiing distance, which refers to the sum of the distances of all 
aircraft from the runway to the gate after landing and vice versa from the 
gate to the runway before takeoff, also increases. It should be noted that 
this increase (or decrease) is not proportional because the objective 
function has multiple components. Also, the results of the sensitivity

To further analyze the impact of integrating GHG emissions in the

unnecessary aircraft movements are reduced. In that context the opti -

walking distances. It highlights the interplay between environmental 
sustainability and passenger experience, emphasizing the need for a 
balanced approach in airport gate assignment strategies.
5. Conclusions
The assignment of airplanes to airport gates has a clear impact on 
operational costs, passenger satisfaction, and environmental sustain -
ability. This paper proposed a model for tackling the associated gate 
assignment problem (GAP), which minimizes passenger walking efforts 
and GHG emissions; the model attempts to improve the carbon footprint 
of an airport and the effort required by travelers to access their con -
necting flight and demonstrates the significant impact of airfield design 
on GHG emissions. A genetic algorithm with a Nash equilibrium control 
was exploited to efficiently and quickly solve the model. The model was 
demonstrated for a hypothetical airport accommodating a realistic flight 
schedule, with results demonstrating its performance against a non- 
optimized airplane-to-gate assignment. In addition, this paper com -
bines passenger comfort with responsiveness by reducing the gate dis -
tance between disembarking from the first flight and boarding the 
second flight.
By modeling each flight as a self-interested agent seeking optimal 
gate assignment, airport managers can ensure that the final solution is 
stable because no single flight can unilaterally change gates to improve 
the overall performance. This approach enhances operational efficiency, 
reduces emissions primary with minimizing total distance, and mini -
mizes gate conflicts, all while ensuring scalable and computationally 
tractable solutions. Importantly, it enables decision-makers to anticipate 
and avoid inefficiencies that could arise from suboptimal or unstable 
gate reassignments, particularly under growing traffic and tighter sus -
tainability goals. With this game-theoretic perspective we want to in -
troduces a new way of balancing competing objectives like passenger 
convenience, environmental impact, and operational constraints in real- 
world airport environments and open a new way for solve airport ori -
ented problems into a more game theoretic framework.
It should be noted, however, that the model has certain limitations; 
additional factors and operational constraints encountered in the liter -
ature could have been included in the model, especially those targeting 
minimizing operational costs and/or maximizing revenues. Also, airline 
policies, such as low-cost airlines or gate leasing, and competition be -
tween airlines ( Sun et al., 2024 ) are not considered in the model. Future 
research could, in that sense, try to balance the contradicting priorities 
of airports, airline companies, and passengers, along with sustainability 
aspects in the GAP formulation, and attempt to describe in greater detail 
passenger and aircraft ground movements affecting those factors.
